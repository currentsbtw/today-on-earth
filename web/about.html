What you are looking at

A map of where the world's news is pointing right now. Each dot is a place that articles published in the last 24 hours are about. A bigger dot means more articles. That is all it means. It is not a map of what matters, and it is not a map of what happened; it is a map of coverage.

Where the articles come from

Every article comes from the GDELT Project, an open dataset that monitors news websites around the world in more than 100 languages and publishes what it found every 15 minutes. We read two of its feeds: the English feed and the translated feed, which covers articles written in other languages. We do not choose outlets. If GDELT saw it, it is here.

How a place is chosen for an article

GDELT lists the places each article mentions, once per mention. We take the city the article mentions most (a city or landmark, in GDELT's terms); when two cities are mentioned equally often, the one mentioned first in the text. An article that mentions no city takes the state or region it mentions most, by the same rule. Articles that only mention countries are counted in the totals but are not drawn anywhere, because a country's centre is not where anything happened. The totals under the Places list tell you how many articles were left unplaced.

Some outlets carry the same place in every article: a footer, a dateline, a sidebar, or their own name read as a place. So for each outlet (a listed network counts as one) with at least 20 articles in the window that name a city or region, a place that appears with the same number of mentions in at least half of those articles is treated as the outlet's fixed block and skipped. The article then takes its next place (we keep its three most-mentioned cities and its most-mentioned region, in that order) or, when none is left, is left unplaced. The same number of mentions is what separates a fixed block from a local outlet that really covers its city, where the count varies. The cost: a wire outlet whose every article carries a one-mention dateline loses that place, even in articles that are about it, and those of its articles that mention no other place are left unplaced.

Places are grouped into hexagonal cells about 100 km across, so "Paris" and its suburbs are one dot. When an article mentions no city and its place is a state or region ("Texas"), its dot sits at that region's centre; that is less wrong than a country's centre, but it is still a centre, and the label tells you so.

What the numbers mean

Articles
Distinct headlines per outlet in the window. An outlet that publishes the same headline under several URLs counts once for it in each place (headlines match by the rule under Distinct and reprints; an article with no title counts once per URL). A network of sites under one owner, listed in the ingest's networks file, counts as one outlet.
Outlets
Distinct website domains; a listed network counts once.
Outlet countries
The countries those outlets are based in, from GDELT's own list of news domains by country. Unknown outlets count as unknown, not as any country.
Languages
The language the article was written in, as GDELT recorded it.
Tone
GDELT's tone score for the article, from negative to positive. We show it as a small mark, never as a word.
Distinct and reprints
Many sites republish the same wire story under the same headline. Within one place, articles whose titles match after we ignore case, punctuation and a trailing outlet name or short label of one or two words (" - Reuters", " | The Star", " - Politics") count as one distinct headline; the copies by other outlets are reprints. A trailing label that one outlet puts on three or more different headlines (a station name, a slogan) is treated like an outlet name. Both numbers are shown and nothing is hidden: the article list shows each headline once, marked with how many reprints it has. Brightness uses the article count, reprints included. A title is shown without the trailing outlet name, short label or learned label this rule ignores; the page at the link keeps its full title, and nothing else in a title is changed.
Heard in N other countries
The countries of the outlets covering a place, not counting the place's own country (the country GDELT most often gives for the place's articles). Outlets with an unknown country are not counted.
Largest news countries
The 20 outlet countries with the most articles in the window, counted over every place. The site names the first 12.
Silent
A country among the 12 largest news countries with no article from an outlet based there in the window, for that place or that story. Silent means that and nothing more. The place's own country is never listed as silent for the place or its stories.
Normal
The median of the place's daily article counts over the last 7 complete UTC days (fewer while less history exists), shown once 3 complete days exist, with today's ratio: "usually about 37 articles a day · today ×2.4". The ratio is (articles + 1) / (normal + 1), with a floor of 3 for the normal, as in "vs. normal" below. A normal of 0 (no articles on more than half of those days) is shown as "usually no articles on most days".

How a place's brightness is decided

A dot's size is the article count on a logarithmic scale, in five steps; the two smallest steps are drawn grey, the rest white. Nothing about the articles' content feeds into it: not what they say, not which outlets wrote them, not what we think. Places with heavy media presence (Washington, London) will usually be big; that is a fact about coverage, and we show it rather than correct it.

A second view, "vs. normal", appears once at least three complete days of history exist. It compares each place's current count with the median of its own daily counts over the last seven days, so a quiet place lighting up stands out and a place that is always busy does not. The dot size is log2((articles + 1) / (normal + 1)) scaled so that eight times normal is full brightness; the floor for "normal" is 3 articles so that tiny places do not explode. Places that are not in the current snapshot are shown with the ordinary brightness and no ratio; a place with no history at all counts as new, with a normal of zero.

Stories

Inside a place, articles are grouped into stories using only what GDELT already tagged for each article: the people, organisations and themes it mentions (its tags), plus the words in its title. An outlet's copies of one headline count as one article here too. Articles are taken oldest first. One joins the group it matches best when it shares two of the group's ten most common tags, or one of those tags and two of the group's twenty most common title words, or four of those words; otherwise it starts a group. Groups whose five most common tags share three are merged. In a place with 50 or more articles, a tag on more than 15% of them (a backdrop, such as "white house" in Washington) is ignored for grouping.

A group of at least 3 articles is a story; a place shows at most 30, largest first. Then each article outside every story joins the story whose twenty most common title words it shares three or more of (ties go to the larger story); it counts in that story but never supplies its headline. The headline shown for a story is the title of one of its grouped articles, chosen because it overlaps most with the others, shown as described under Distinct and reprints. We never write a headline. Articles that fit no story are listed below, unfiltered.

The daily broadcast

The Signals section (the right column on wide screens, the first section below the map on narrower ones) is a daily broadcast: six fixed slots, each filled by one story from the last 24 hours under a published rule. A filled slot shows an outlet's own headline, verbatim apart from the trailing outlet name or label (see Distinct and reprints), and the story's coverage numbers. Nothing in it is written or summarised by us or by a model. The sentence under each headline is a fixed template filled with the story's own numbers ("One story over Nairobi: 214 articles from 61 outlets in 19 languages."), so they count that story, not everything written about the place.

Every slot needs a story with at least 3 distinct headlines, so one article echoed across many sites never fills a slot, and the story must have a headline. "Heard across the planet" also needs the place's own country to be known. The slots, in order:

Loudest signal
The story with the most independent voices: distinct headlines, counting at most one per outlet (so neither syndication networks nor one outlet's many copies win).
Heard across the planet
The story with the most countries other than the place's own; at least 10 outlets.
Rising
The place most above its own normal (articles divided by the larger of its normal and 3); its story is that place's story with the most outlets. Needs 3 complete days of history; at least 20 articles.
Most positive tone
The most positive mean tone among stories with at least 10 outlets, 3 countries and 10 articles.
Ordinary life
The most outlets among stories where at most a quarter of the articles carry conflict, politics, crime or disaster themes; at least 10 outlets and 2 countries.
Faint signal
The most outlets from a place that is usually quiet (a normal of at most 3 articles a day, including places with no history). Needs 3 complete days of history; at least 5 outlets and 2 countries.

The broadcast page, its feed and each place's feed carry the same fixed sentences, headlines and numbers as the site, nothing more.

The four theme families, in full, from GDELT's own theme tags. A name ending in * covers every theme that starts with it.

Conflict
ARMEDCONFLICT, MILITARY, TERROR, WMD, REBELLION, REBELS, SEIGE, BLOCKADE, CEASEFIRE, KILL, WOUND, UNREST_*.
Politics
ELECTION, LEGISLATION, GENERAL_GOVERNMENT, LEADER, GOV_*, USPEC_POLITICS_GENERAL1, POLITICAL_TURMOIL, RESIGNATION, APPOINTMENT, CONSTITUTIONAL, DEMOCRACY, NEGOTIATIONS, ALLIANCE, SANCTIONS, SOVEREIGNTY.
Crime
ARREST, TRIAL, SOC_GENERALCRIME, CRIME_*, KIDNAP, RAPE, DRUG_TRADE, ORGANIZED_CRIME, CORRUPTION, CYBER_ATTACK.
Disaster
NATURAL_DISASTER_*, MANMADE_DISASTER_* (except MANMADE_DISASTER_IMPLIED), DISASTER_*, EVACUATION.

GDELT's CRISISLEX themes are deliberately not used. They are word-list matches, not topics, and they tag about a third of all articles, restaurant reviews and earnings calls included.

Each story fills at most one slot, taken in the order above. Within a slot, ties go to the story with more articles, then the earlier story, then the place; the loudest slot first breaks ties by more outlets. An empty slot says why: not enough history yet, with the date it can start, or no story met the rule.

The choice of slots and their thresholds is a framing decision, made once and published here. The filled slots also close the daily clip, each as a card before the final one.

What this site does not do

  • It does not rank, recommend or summarise. The daily broadcast's slots are maxima under the rules above, computed from the numbers alone; nobody picks them.
  • It does not host articles or videos. Links go to the outlet. When an outlet embedded a YouTube video, we show its title and outlet as a plain card; nothing is requested from YouTube until you press play, and the player is YouTube's privacy-enhanced one.
  • It does not track who is looking. The observers count on the map page is the number of browsers that checked in during the last 75 seconds, each known only by a random id made for that tab; no address, cookie or device detail is kept, and nothing is logged. When the counter is off, the label reads plain "Observers".
  • It does not know what is true. A place can light up because of a rumour.
  • It does not see everything. GDELT misses outlets, geocodes wrongly sometimes, and sees more of the English-language web than any other. Dark areas mean no coverage was found, which is not the same as nothing happening.
  • It has no editor and no feed. What you see is the same for everyone.

Replay

The Replay section (the right column on wide screens, opened by REPLAY in the top line; below the map on narrower ones) replays the last 24 hours in 15-minute steps. Each step is a snapshot of the same rolling window at that time.

The replay bars show how many articles were new in each step, not the window's total. An article is new in a step when it was not in the previous snapshot's window. A snapshot made after a restart, or with no previous snapshot in the 24 hours before it, has no count and is drawn as unknown, as are snapshots made before 22 October 2026.

GDELT publishes in 15-minute files, and the translated feed arrives about an hour after the English one, so the number of new articles per step rises and falls with that rhythm.

Freshness

A new snapshot is built every 15 minutes. The top line shows the time of the one you are looking at, in UTC. Right after a restart the window is shorter, and an open place's coverage line says how many hours it covers.

Earthquakes

An optional layer draws earthquakes of magnitude 2.5 and above from the last 24 hours, straight from the United States Geological Survey's public feed. It is off by default, and nothing is requested from USGS until you turn it on. Rings are sized by magnitude; they are not part of the coverage dots and never affect them.

Data, code, attribution

The site is open source. Map outlines are from Natural Earth (public domain). News data is from the GDELT Project, used under its open terms with attribution. Earthquake data is from the USGS Earthquake Hazards Program.

How Today On Earth works

What you are looking at

A map of where the world's news is pointing right now. Each dot is a place that articles published in the last 24 hours are about. A bigger dot means more articles. That is all it means. It is not a map of what matters, and it is not a map of what happened; it is a map of coverage.

Where the articles come from

Every article comes from the GDELT Project, an open dataset that monitors news websites around the world in more than 100 languages and publishes what it found every 15 minutes. We read two of its feeds: the English feed and the translated feed, which covers articles written in other languages. We do not choose outlets. If GDELT saw it, it is here.

How a place is chosen for an article

GDELT lists the places each article mentions. We take the first place that is more specific than a whole country: a city, a region, a landmark. Articles that only mention countries are counted in the totals but are not drawn anywhere, because a country's centre is not where anything happened. The header tells you how many articles were left unplaced for this reason.

Places are grouped into hexagonal cells about 100 km across, so "Paris" and its suburbs are one dot.

What the numbers mean

Articles
Distinct URLs in the window.
Outlets
Distinct website domains.
Countries
The countries those outlets are based in, from GDELT's own list of news domains by country. Unknown outlets count as unknown, not as any country.
Languages
The language the article was written in, as GDELT recorded it.
Tone
GDELT's tone score for the article, from negative to positive. We show it as a small mark, never as a word.
Distinct and reprints
Many sites republish the same wire story under the same headline. Within one place, articles whose titles match after we ignore case, punctuation and a trailing outlet name (" - Reuters", " | The Star") count as one distinct headline; the others are reprints. Both numbers are shown and nothing is hidden: the article list shows each headline once, marked with how many reprints it has. Brightness still uses the article count, reprints included.
Heard in N other countries
The countries of the outlets covering a place, not counting the place's own country (the country GDELT most often gives for the place's articles). Outlets with an unknown country are not counted.

How "hot" is decided

A dot's size is the article count on a logarithmic scale, in five steps; the two smallest steps are drawn grey, the rest white. Nothing about the articles' content feeds into it: not what they say, not which outlets wrote them, not what we think. Places with heavy media presence (Washington, London) will usually be big; that is a fact about coverage, and we show it rather than correct it.

A second view, "vs. normal", appears once at least three complete days of history exist. It compares each place's current count with the median of its own daily counts over the last seven days, so a quiet place lighting up stands out and a place that is always busy does not. The dot size is log2((articles + 1) / (normal + 1)) scaled so that eight times normal is full brightness; the floor for "normal" is 3 articles so that tiny places do not explode. Places that are not in the current snapshot are shown with the ordinary brightness and no ratio; a place with no history at all counts as new, with a normal of zero.

Stories

Inside a place, articles are grouped into stories using only what GDELT already tagged for each article: the people, organisations and themes it mentions, plus the words in its title. The headline shown for a story is the title of one of its articles, chosen because it overlaps most with the others. We never write or edit a headline. Articles that fit no group are listed below, unfiltered.

The daily broadcast

The Signals button opens a daily broadcast: six fixed slots, each filled by one story from the last 24 hours under a published rule. A filled slot shows an outlet's own headline, verbatim, and the story's coverage numbers. Nothing in it is written or summarised by us or by a model. The sentence above each headline is a fixed template filled with the story's own numbers ("One story over Nairobi: 214 articles from 61 outlets in 19 languages."), so they count that story, not everything written about the place.

Every slot needs a story with at least 3 distinct headlines, so one article echoed across many sites never fills a slot, and the story must have a headline. "Heard across the planet" also needs the place's own country to be known. The slots, in order:

Loudest signal
The story with the most independent voices: distinct headlines, counting at most one per outlet (so neither syndication networks nor one outlet's many copies win).
Heard across the planet
The story with the most countries other than the place's own; at least 10 outlets.
Rising
The place most above its own normal (articles divided by the larger of its normal and 3); its story is that place's story with the most outlets. Needs 3 complete days of history; at least 20 articles.
Most positive tone
The most positive mean tone among stories with at least 10 outlets, 3 countries and 10 articles.
Ordinary life
The most outlets among stories where at most a quarter of the articles carry conflict, politics, crime or disaster themes; at least 10 outlets and 2 countries.
Faint signal
The most outlets from a place that is usually quiet (a normal of at most 3 articles a day, including places with no history). Needs 3 complete days of history; at least 5 outlets and 2 countries.

The four theme families, in full, from GDELT's own theme tags. A name ending in * covers every theme that starts with it.

Conflict
ARMEDCONFLICT, MILITARY, TERROR, WMD, REBELLION, REBELS, SEIGE, BLOCKADE, CEASEFIRE, KILL, WOUND, UNREST_*.
Politics
ELECTION, LEGISLATION, GENERAL_GOVERNMENT, LEADER, GOV_*, USPEC_POLITICS_GENERAL1, POLITICAL_TURMOIL, RESIGNATION, APPOINTMENT, CONSTITUTIONAL, DEMOCRACY, NEGOTIATIONS, ALLIANCE, SANCTIONS, SOVEREIGNTY.
Crime
ARREST, TRIAL, SOC_GENERALCRIME, CRIME_*, KIDNAP, RAPE, DRUG_TRADE, ORGANIZED_CRIME, CORRUPTION, CYBER_ATTACK.
Disaster
NATURAL_DISASTER_*, MANMADE_DISASTER_* (except MANMADE_DISASTER_IMPLIED), DISASTER_*, EVACUATION.

GDELT's CRISISLEX themes are deliberately not used. They are word-list matches, not topics, and they tag about a third of all articles, restaurant reviews and earnings calls included.

Each story fills at most one slot, taken in the order above. Within a slot, ties go to the story with more articles, then the earlier story, then the place; the loudest slot first breaks ties by more outlets. An empty slot says why: not enough history yet, with the date it can start, or no story met the rule.

The choice of slots and their thresholds is a framing decision, made once and published here. The filled slots also close the daily clip, each as a card before the final one.

What this site does not do

  • It does not rank, recommend or summarise. The daily broadcast's slots are maxima under the rules above, computed from the numbers alone; nobody picks them.
  • It does not host articles or videos. Links go to the outlet. When an outlet embedded a YouTube video, we show its title and outlet as a plain card; nothing is requested from YouTube until you press play, and the player is YouTube's privacy-enhanced one.
  • It does not track who is looking. The observers count on the map page is the number of browsers that checked in during the last 75 seconds, each known only by a random id made for that tab; no address, cookie or device detail is kept, and nothing is logged. When the counter is off, the label reads plain "Observers".
  • It does not know what is true. A place can light up because of a rumour.
  • It does not see everything. GDELT misses outlets, geocodes wrongly sometimes, and sees more of the English-language web than any other. Dark areas mean no coverage was found, which is not the same as nothing happening.
  • It has no editor and no feed. What you see is the same for everyone.

Replay

The scrubber under the map replays the last 24 hours in 15-minute steps. Each step is a snapshot of the same rolling window at that time.

Freshness

A new snapshot is built every 15 minutes. The header shows the time of the one you are looking at, in UTC. Right after a restart the window is shorter and the header says so.

Earthquakes

An optional layer draws earthquakes of magnitude 2.5 and above from the last 24 hours, straight from the United States Geological Survey's public feed. It is off by default, and nothing is requested from USGS until you turn it on. Rings are sized by magnitude; they are not part of the news heat and never affect it.

Data, code, attribution

The site is open source. Map outlines are from Natural Earth (public domain). News data is from the GDELT Project, used under its open terms with attribution. Earthquake data is from the USGS Earthquake Hazards Program.
